\section{Related literature}

The research technology follows the endogenous- and semi-endogenous-growth
tradition in which resources devoted to ideas determine productivity growth
\citep{romer1990,aghionhowitt1992,jones1995}. When human researchers remain
essential, population growth can anchor long-run innovation. I ask when that
anchor disappears because AI services can substitute for research labor. Unlike
the standard formulation, I separate substitution between research inputs from
returns to their current scale.

\citet{davidsonetal2026} provide the closest feedback architecture. They model
innovation as a network of technological and economic links, classify growth
using a feedback matrix, and distinguish nonrival AI capability from rival
compute. Their quantitative application fixes saving and resource-allocation
shares and treats automation as exogenous. I reduce the technology network to one
capability stock and place its production and research loops inside a
decentralized Ramsey economy. Saving, labor allocation, compute use, prices, and
factor payments are then equilibrium outcomes. My balanced-growth feedback
condition is a scalar analogue of their matrix criterion, not an application of
their general theorem.

This distinction also separates my research-curvature restriction from Davidson
et al.'s feedback condition. Their condition determines whether technological
and economic feedback can sustain explosive growth. Mine prevents a developer
from obtaining unbounded value through an arbitrarily large research outlay on a
fixed horizon. Neither condition alone proves equilibrium existence.

Several papers study related AI-growth mechanisms. Extending automation to idea
production can generate rapid growth, while tasks that remain difficult to
automate preserve bottlenecks \citep{aghionjonesjones2019}. Other work develops
empirical diagnostics for an economic singularity, distinguishes automation in
production from automation in R\&D, or studies AI directly as an input into
research \citep{nordhaus2021,trammellkorinek2023,jones2025rd}.
\citet{erdiletal2025gate} combine AI development, automation, endogenous
investment, adjustment costs, and semi-endogenous growth in a quantitative
scenario model. They abstract from market prices and market structure. I instead
focus on how decentralized prices and two substitution margins determine the
feedback and the allocation of resources.

The model's compute accounting is informed by \citet{korinekmckelvey2026}. They
distinguish physical compute, inference, training and R\&D, algorithms, and model
capital. I retain the distinction between compute used for inference and compute
used for research, but combine model quality and algorithmic efficiency in one
capability stock. This is a tractability choice, not a relabeling of their full
accounting framework.

The production side connects to the literature on substitution and growth
\citep{pitchford1960,jonesmanuelli1990,rebelo1991}. Whether the relevant
elasticity is below or above one determines whether the slower-growing input
remains a bottleneck. Empirical work shows why the elasticity should not be
assumed mechanically: equipment can complement labor even when software is a
gross substitute \citep{duffypapageorgiou2000,aumshin2024}. These estimates do
not calibrate the AI-service elasticity in my model, but they support separating
software-like services from undifferentiated physical capital.

\citet{jonestonetti2026} provide the closest dynamic precedent for the delayed
takeoff. In their task model, slowly improving human tasks remain weak links, so
aggregate paths can look similar for a long time before diverging sharply. An
earlier illustration in \citet{aghionjonesjones2019} also produces prolonged low
growth while automation continues, although their automation path is exogenous.
These task models and my CES formulation are not equivalent. Their state variable
is the set of tasks assigned to capital; mine is the service flow produced by AI
and combined with labor. The common mechanism is that a complementary human input
can delay or prevent AI dominance.

The distributional results build on the automation literature. Task automation
displaces labor, while new human tasks can reinstate labor demand
\citep{acemoglurestrepo2019}. Wages and labor's income share need not move in the
same direction when output changes rapidly
\citep{trammellkorinek2023,autorkausik2026}. Most directly,
\citet{restrepo2025agi} shows that if compute can perform all bottleneck work,
compute-driven growth can make labor asymptotically inessential and drive its
income share to zero. I do not claim either labor displacement or a vanishing
labor share as new.

My contribution is to derive these outcomes in a decentralized economy that
separates conventional capital, production and research labor, inference and
research compute, and developer profit. This accounting matters because the
final-good firm's payment for AI services is gross revenue, not monopoly profit,
and compute expenditure is a resource use rather than a final income recipient.
The model does not assign the upstream compute payments to hardware producers,
energy suppliers, workers, or owners of additional capital.

Finally, the integrated developer connects the analysis to work on AI market
structure \citep{korinekvipra2025,gans2024,demireretal2025}. The developer
charges a markup on production AI services and internalizes the future profit
created by better capability. I hold this market structure fixed to isolate the
technology; comparing it with monopolistic competition remains a natural
extension.
