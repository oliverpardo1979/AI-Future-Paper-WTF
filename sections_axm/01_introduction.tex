\section{Introduction}

Advances in artificial intelligence raise both hopes and fears. One hope is
that increasingly capable AI could generate unprecedented economic prosperity.
One fear is that workers who depend on labor income could lose economic power
relative to the owners of capital and AI. Can unprecedented prosperity coexist
with a vanishing labor income share? I study this question in a relatively
parsimonious endogenous-growth model. In the equilibria established here,
complementarity preserves a positive labor share, while unit elasticity permits
sustained growth with a constant production-labor share. Rising real wages and
a vanishing labor share coexist on a conditional gross-substitutes branch of
the necessary conditions. Because I do not establish that branch as an
infinite-horizon equilibrium, I do not present it as a simulated equilibrium
trajectory.

The mechanism operates through two feedback loops. When current capability
makes compute more productive in both production and research, better AI raises
current output and helps create still better AI. Following
\citet{davidsonetal2026}, I distinguish a technological loop, from capability to
research productivity, and an economic loop, from capability to output and
therefore to the resources available for investment and research.

I study these loops in a decentralized endogenous-growth model. Raw compute has
two uses. Inference compute produces AI production services for final-good firms.
Research compute produces AI research services used together with human
researchers to improve AI capability. An integrated developer sells AI production services and
chooses how much to invest in capability. A representative household owns
physical capital and the developer, supplies labor, and chooses consumption.

Two substitution margins organize the analysis. The first measures how easily
AI production services replace labor in final production. The second measures how easily
AI research services replace human researchers. Research scale is a
separate margin: expanding all current research inputs has decreasing returns.
The formal model introduces these elasticities and the research-curvature
parameter only after presenting the relevant technologies.

The distinction between raw compute and capability-adjusted AI services is essential. A unit of
hardware, energy, and datacenter time is not a unit of intelligence. Capability
determines the service flow produced by a unit of compute. I therefore model both
AI production and AI research services as capability times the corresponding compute
input. This one-capability representation is a tractable version of the broader
capability--compute architecture in \citet{davidsonetal2026}. It is also
consistent with the distinction in \citet{korinekmckelvey2026} between physical
compute, training and R\&D activity, algorithms, inference output, and model
capital. My capability stock combines model quality and algorithmic efficiency;
my research-compute input includes training, post-training, experimentation,
synthetic-data generation, and automated algorithmic research.

\subsection{Empirical motivation}

Available evidence supports separating compute from the services it produces,
but it does not identify the model's substitution elasticities. According to
\citet{korinekmckelvey2026}, U.S. AI compute expenditure rose from \$36.9 billion
in 2023 to \$219.2 billion in 2025, while estimated raw capacity rose from 1.09
million to 10.7 million NVIDIA H100-equivalent units. Their quality-adjusted
indexes of inference and training output increased much faster. These indexes
are model-based estimates, not national-account aggregates, but they show why
compute and capability-adjusted AI services should not be treated as the same object.

Compute also remains a real resource. The International Energy Agency estimates
that global data-center electricity use rose from 415 TWh in 2024 to 485 TWh in
2025 and projects about 950 TWh in 2030 \citep{iea2025,iea2026}. Normalizing the
unit cost of compute therefore changes units; it does not remove compute from the
economy's resource constraint.

Evidence on automated AI research is more limited. Human experts substantially
outperformed the best AI agent in the original 32-hour RE-Bench evaluation
\citep{metrrebench2024}. A later evaluation on five selected tasks found that
aggregated shorter Claude 3.7 runs could match the median score reached by humans
at eight hours \citep{metrclaude2025}. The tasks, models, tools, and time budgets
differ across the two exercises. They motivate the possibility that AI can
substitute for some research labor, but they do not measure that elasticity in
the economy. I therefore treat the numerical exercises as scenarios, not
forecasts or calibrations to these studies.

\subsection{Results and contribution}

I obtain three regimes. When AI production services and labor are complements in final
production, labor remains a bottleneck. Along the regular finite-rate
balanced-growth paths characterized below, AI capability generates no per-capita
growth beyond exogenous labor productivity, and labor retains a positive income
share. In the reported case without exogenous productivity growth, output per
person and real wages approach constants. Capability can
nevertheless keep improving, and AI research services can receive almost all
research expenditure when they substitute for human researchers.

At unit elasticity in final production, positive balanced growth requires the
feedback from capability to production and research to remain below an explicit
threshold. The threshold is necessary but not sufficient for an equilibrium to
exist. It is the scalar counterpart of the feedback-matrix condition in
\citet{davidsonetal2026}. In the automated-research benchmark, I give explicit
conditions for existence and show that the calibrated balanced path has the
saddle-path structure needed to select consumption and the value of capability
from nearby predetermined stocks. Around unit elasticity, a nondegenerate
boundary-value solution of the dated equilibrium conditions persists on every
fixed finite window, while economically material departures accumulate on a time scale of order
\(1/|\sigma_{XL}-1|\).

When AI production services are gross substitutes for labor and become dominant, unbounded
capability makes the output--capital ratio and the interest rate unbounded. A
finite-rate balanced-growth path is then impossible. I characterize a
conditional branch of the necessary conditions on which output, capital,
consumption, and capability accelerate and output becomes unbounded in finite
model time. Labor's income share converges to zero, although the real wage can
rise because output grows even faster. In the automated-research benchmark,
bounded capability cannot coexist with a bounded interest rate. These results
reject the two regular long-run continuations used by the algorithm. They do
not yet eliminate bounded capability with unbounded interest-rate spikes or an
unbounded-capability path whose growth is sufficiently irregular.

To study this boundary without relabeling a truncated path as an equilibrium,
I introduce a finite capability frontier \(\overline B\) as an analytical
regularization. For every fixed frontier, capability remains below
\(\overline B\) at every finite date. Conditional on a regular frontier
approach, the terminal equilibrium has a sharp division: a low frontier leaves
effective labor essential, whereas a high frontier produces an AI-dominated
\(AK\)-type regime with a vanishing labor share. As
\(\overline B\to\infty\), the capped equations converge to the uncapped
equations on bounded pre-singular intervals, but the terminal interest and
growth rates diverge. The finite-window and long-run limits therefore cannot
be interchanged, and the regularization does not by itself establish an
uncapped infinite-horizon equilibrium. Numerically, I continue the stable
manifold from a moderate finite frontier to the paper's initial stocks and
verify the complete equilibrium-admission conditions. When I enlarge the
frontier substantially, the canonical BVP still converges, but the sufficient
optimality condition for the developer fails along the transition. The finite
frontier therefore establishes a fixed-frontier equilibrium, not yet a sequence
of equilibria converging to the uncapped economy.

I also separate labor displacement from the uses of the displaced
share. The outer production technology keeps the final-good firm's gross capital
payment fixed. A fall in labor income therefore raises gross payments for AI
production services, not developer profit one for one. The developer uses that revenue to
pay for inference compute, human research, and research compute before
distributing the residual profit. This decomposition extends the established
result that sufficiently extensive automation can reduce labor's share
\citep{acemoglurestrepo2019,trammellkorinek2023,restrepo2025agi}. It does not
determine interpersonal inequality because the model does not specify who owns
the upstream compute sector.

My main contribution is to determine production, research, saving, prices, and
the allocation of compute jointly in a decentralized Ramsey economy with an
integrated AI developer. Davidson et al. provide the closest feedback
architecture, but hold key saving and resource shares fixed. The quantitative
framework in \citet{erdiletal2025gate} combines AI development, automation,
investment, and growth but abstracts from market prices and market structure. I
focus on how equilibrium prices and the two substitution margins govern growth,
wages, factor payments, compute use, and developer profit.

The analytical claims are conditional on the assumptions stated with each
result. Every reported simulation passes the paper's equilibrium-admission
rule, including a regime-specific terminal, sufficient optimality conditions,
and both transversality conditions. The gross-substitutes free-boundary
calculation fails that rule and is retained only as a diagnostic of the
conditional analytical branch.
