\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:rewrite-conclusion}

I develop an endogenous-growth model to study how substitution between
effective AI production services and human labor shapes economic growth,
wages, interest rates, and the functional distribution of income when AI
research is autonomous. The main result is that the long-run economic role of
human labor depends critically on whether AI can replace labor's contribution
to production.

When AI and labor are complements, human labor remains a bottleneck.
Improvements in AI efficiency raise productive capacity but do not permanently
increase the growth of output per person or real wages beyond the exogenous
growth of labor productivity. Labor therefore retains a positive share of
income. The same broad conclusion holds under unit elasticity: AI can support
sustained growth, but human labor remains economically essential and continues
to receive a positive share of output.

The outcome changes when AI and labor are sufficiently substitutable. Gross
substitution is necessary but not sufficient: labor remains a bottleneck while
AI services are not productive enough to replace its contribution. Once AI
becomes sufficiently efficient, however, capital and effective AI production
services can expand faster than effective labor. Output per person, real wages,
and the interest rate then grow faster than in the labor-bottleneck regime.

The distributional result is not that workers necessarily become poorer. In
the AI-dominated regime, real wages continue to rise, but output per person
grows even faster. Labor's income share therefore converges to zero. Because
the gross capital share is fixed by the production technology, this
transformation reallocates the remainder of output from labor toward the AI
industry. AI-industry revenue should not be confused with monopoly profit: it
finances inference and research before the residual is distributed to the
developer's owner.

The transition can be as important as the long-run outcome. The numerical
equilibrium paths show that the economy may remain for a long time on a
moderate-growth trajectory before output accelerates and labor's income share
falls sharply. A prolonged period in which little appears to change therefore
need not imply that labor will remain economically essential. The timing in the
simulations is illustrative, however, because the speed of AI research is not
estimated.

The analysis points to three lines of future work. A task-based extension with
heterogeneous workers, new human tasks, and a continuing role for human
researchers could combine the displacement and reinstatement mechanisms of
\citet{acemoglurestrepo2019} with the research-automation network of
\citet{davidsonetal2026}. An explicit upstream compute sector, alternative
market structures, and heterogeneous asset ownership could build on
\citet{korinekmckelvey2026} and \citet{korinekvipra2025} and translate changes
in functional income shares into household inequality and economic power.
Finally, a more complete existence analysis could replace bounded AI
efficiency with an endogenous or unbounded process and determine whether the
resulting economy admits an infinite-horizon equilibrium. This question is
related to, but not resolved by, the explosive-growth mechanisms in
\citet{nordhaus2021} and \citet{davidsonetal2026}.

The central question is whether human labor remains sufficiently essential for
wages to keep pace with the economy as a whole.
