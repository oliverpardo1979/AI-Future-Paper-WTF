% BEGIN REVISED CONCLUSION -- active
I endogenize investment in AI research to study how private incentives
determine the pace of recursive self-improvement. Even when research is
autonomous, compute requires resources that compete with consumption and
physical-capital accumulation. Algorithms and model weights, by contrast,
are nonrival: using them does not deplete them. Following \citet{romer1990},
I use monopoly rents to make investment in this knowledge privately
profitable. This is an assumption about how innovation is financed, not
a claim that nonrival knowledge requires a single producer.

With a finite upper bound on AI efficiency, permanently faster wage growth
requires sufficient substitution between AI and labor and sufficiently high
attainable efficiency. In this AI-dominated regime, output and AI revenue
per worker grow faster than wages, so labor's income share vanishes even
as wages rise. When labor remains a production bottleneck, growth in output
per worker and wages instead returns to the exogenous rate of labor-augmenting
technological progress. The upper bound matters for this distinction:
without it, the unit-elasticity case can sustain faster output and wage
growth with a positive labor share.

By construction, the gross remuneration of physical capital receives a
fixed share of output. I distinguish this income from AI revenue, which
pays for inference and research compute before yielding net profit to
the developer. An expanding AI industry therefore need not translate
one for one into higher developer profit. Nor does a profitable AI industry
necessarily imply permanently faster growth: it can coexist with a labor
bottleneck.

The numerical simulations show that early growth can look similar across
the two regimes. Research productivity changes both the initial allocation
of resources and the delay before their paths diverge, but not the limiting
outcomes of the capped regimes studied here. I therefore place more weight
on the conditional long-run results than on takeoff dates. Gradual adoption
and delays in expanding compute capacity would help assess how much of the
initial adjustment reflects the unanticipated activation of RSI and the
absence of installation costs. \citet{erdiletal2025gate} provide an example
of modeling investment adjustment costs.

Permanent monopoly is another important limitation. As
\citet{korinekvipra2025} explain, access to better AI can improve a leading
developer's research and widen its advantage. This motivates examining
concentration, but does not establish that one firm will eventually
dominate. Allowing entry, competing developers, and knowledge diffusion
would help determine how competition changes private RSI investment,
prices, and the division of its gains.

Finally, AI-dominated growth can permanently raise the net return on
physical capital, raising questions about wealth accumulation and
intergenerational distribution. \citet{mollrachelrestrepo2022} show how
automation can increase wealth concentration through higher returns.
I do not establish that effect here: growth rises alongside the interest
rate, and the representative household owns both capital and the AI
developer. Heterogeneous ownership and inheritance would be needed to study
these distributional questions. The question for labor remains whether
it is sufficiently essential for wages to keep pace with the economy
as a whole.
