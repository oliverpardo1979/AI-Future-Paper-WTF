\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:rewrite-conclusion}

This paper endogenizes the decision on AI research investment, which in turn determines the pace at which AI evolves. Even if AI reaches RSI, which seems either inevitable or has already happened, computing tasks demand resources that rival with consumption and capital formation. But AI also functions with inputs that are non-rival goods like algorithms and model weights. I assumed monopoly rents to give incentives to invest in these non-rival goods. In practice, firms are still competing in developing algorithims and training mahcines and keeping a secret on their model weights and even trying to infer (if not subtlety stealing) those weights. Whether this competition will eventually lead to an actual monopoly it is still an open question, but it does not seem to far-fetched to claim that the advantages of a particular model over the rest will be so big that the industry could succumb to a monopoly. This is in fact one of the reasons behind the fierce competition between companies and even countries.

By construction, the capital share in income is fixed. But notably, AI may lead to a permanent increase in the return to wealth in the form of a higher interest rate, which raises concerns regarding the impact on the intergenerational distribution of wealth not covered in this paper. The paper discusses the distribution of the rest of the income between workers and the AI industry revenue. It’s only when AI revenue outpaces wages that wages can grow permanently faster. At the same time the fear of a monopoly that absorbs an increasing share of income sounds like a legitimate concern, even if it carries along a permanent acceleration of wages. Still, these concerns rely on the complementarity between labor and IA. If there is no bound on AI capabilities, the question becomes whether human labor will remain sufficiently essential for wages to keep pace with the rest of the economy. %Workers become better paid while receiving a declining share of aggregate income.

Above it, higher attainable efficiency raises long-run growth and the interest rate. The wage-growth premium over the exogenous rate equals the output-per-worker growth premium divided by the substitution elasticity, which measures how much of the growth acceleration reaches wages.
