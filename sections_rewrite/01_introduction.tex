\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:rewrite-introduction}

Advances in artificial intelligence raise both hopes and fears. One hope is
that increasingly capable AI could generate unprecedented economic prosperity.
One fear is that workers who depend on labor income could lose economic power
relative to the owners of capital and AI as labor's income share falls. This
fear does not require real wages to decline: workers may receive a smaller
share of a much larger economy even as their wages rise. Can unprecedented
prosperity coexist with a vanishing labor income share?

I study this question by extending the Ramsey--Cass--Koopmans model. Competitive
firms produce the final good with capital, effective labor, and effective AI
production services. A monopolistic AI developer supplies those services and
uses effective AI research services to improve AI efficiency, creating
recursive self-improvement. AI efficiency improves endogenously but approaches
a finite frontier. I ask how the substitutability between AI and labor shapes
economic growth, real wages, interest rates, and the distribution of output.

The mechanism combines two feedback loops. A technological loop runs from AI
efficiency to AI research: greater efficiency produces more effective research
services from a given amount of research compute and thereby helps create still
better AI. An economic loop runs from AI efficiency to output and back to
research: better AI raises current production and expands the resources that
can finance further improvements. This distinction follows
\citet{davidsonetal2026}. In my model, household saving, capital accumulation,
inference compute, research compute, and the developer's investment decision
are determined jointly. Whether these feedbacks raise long-run growth depends
on whether effective labor continues to constrain production.

I first characterize the labor-bottleneck regime. When AI and labor are
complements, or when they are substitutes but attainable AI efficiency is not
high enough, effective labor remains essential. Along the equilibria I
construct, output per person and the real wage then grow at the exogenous rate
of labor-augmenting technological progress. The net interest rate converges to
its Ramsey value, and labor retains a positive share of output. Unit elasticity
also belongs to this labor-supported class. Greater AI efficiency can change
levels and transition dynamics without permanently raising growth above the
exogenous rate.

The outcome changes when AI and labor are sufficiently substitutable and AI
efficiency can reach a sufficiently high level. Gross substitution is therefore
necessary but not sufficient for the economy to become AI-dominated. Above an
explicit threshold, capital and effective AI production services can grow
faster than effective labor. The growth rates of output per person and the real
wage then exceed their labor-bottleneck benchmarks, as does the level of the
interest rate. The real wage still grows, but output per person grows faster,
so labor's income share converges to zero. Moreover, the long-run growth rate
and the interest rate increase with the level of AI efficiency that the economy
can attain.

The transition need not reveal this long-run regime quickly. I compute four
equilibrium paths that share the same parameters, AI-efficiency frontier, and
predetermined initial stocks, varying only the elasticity between AI and labor.
In the illustrative AI-dominated case, output per person is still growing at
only $1.062\%$ and labor receives $60.5\%$ of output after 500 model years. By
year 1,500, growth has risen to $2.797\%$ and labor's share has fallen to
$14.5\%$. A long period of apparently moderate growth can therefore precede a
sharp acceleration and a large redistribution of output. These model years are
not calendar forecasts: the duration of the transition depends on an
illustrative research calibration.

The paper's contribution is to characterize these growth and distributional
outcomes jointly in a decentralized economy with endogenous saving, prices,
compute allocation, AI investment, and a profit-maximizing developer. I give
sufficient conditions for nearby infinite-horizon equilibrium trajectories,
rather than only balanced-growth candidates, and report only numerical paths
that satisfy the equilibrium-admission conditions. Because the representative
household owns both capital and the AI developer, the model characterizes the
functional distribution of output, not household inequality or political
power. The existence results are local in the initial stocks and apply to each
fixed finite AI-efficiency frontier; they do not establish equilibrium from
arbitrary initial conditions or for an unbounded frontier.
