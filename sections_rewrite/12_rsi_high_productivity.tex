\subsection{Higher research productivity}
\label{subsec:rewrite-rsi-half-decline}

I now compare the transitional dynamics for the four elasticities under
relatively high research productivity, $\chi=7.5$. Research productivity
affects the transition, but not the limiting growth rates characterized in
Section~\ref{sec:rewrite-regimes}. All four economies inherit the pre-RSI
balanced growth paths described in Section~\ref{subsec:rewrite-rsi-activation}.
Table~\ref{tab:rewrite-rsi-parameters} reports their common parameters and
elasticity-specific initial capital stocks. I examine quantities first,
then wages and prices, and finally income shares.

I use Figure~\ref{fig:rewrite-rsi-half-accumulation} to compare the growth
of $Y/(AL)$, $X/(AL)$, and $K/(AL)$ in panels A, B, and C, respectively.
The plotted rates subtract effective-labor growth, $n+\gamma$, and are
expressed in percent per year. Thus zero means growth at the rate of
effective labor, not constant output or capital. The upper row shows two
pre-event years and the first ten years after activation; the lower row
follows the same paths from year 10 to year 500. Throughout the three
figures, green dashed, black solid, orange dotted, and blue dash-dotted
lines denote $\sigma=0.90,1,1.10,1.50$, respectively. The vertical line
marks activation, and black dotted horizontal lines mark the analytical
limits for $\sigma=1.50$. Vertical scales differ across time windows.

In the upper row, AI services respond much more strongly than output.
At $\sigma=1.50$, $X/(AL)$ initially grows at $80.8\%$ per year,
whereas $Y/(AL)$ grows at $3.5\%$. These are instantaneous growth rates,
not jumps in levels. Research begins to raise $B$, allowing more services
to be obtained from compute through $X=BU$ in
equation~\eqref{eq:rewrite-ai-service}. Final production cannot expand
at the same rate because it also requires capital and labor.
Initial growth of $Y/(AL)$ is smaller at the other elasticities, reaching
only $0.6\%$ at $\sigma=0.90$.
Panel C shows a different initial response: $K/(AL)$ falls in every
case. Households increase consumption at activation while the developer
starts spending on research. With current output and inference use
unchanged at that instant, the resource constraint
\eqref{eq:rewrite-resource} requires lower investment. Capital accumulation
subsequently recovers as output expands.

The lower row separates the temporary growth response from the long-run
regime. At $\sigma=0.90,1,1.10$, all three normalized growth rates decline
toward zero. This is the labor-bottleneck outcome in
Proposition~\ref{prop:rewrite-equilibrium-regimes}: output per person
eventually grows at $\gamma=1.0\%$. At $\sigma=1.50$, output and capital
growth instead rise further before declining toward a positive limit.
As explained in Section~\ref{subsec:rewrite-equilibrium-existence},
sufficient substitution and a sufficiently high efficiency bound allow
capital and inference compute to expand together without being limited
by effective-labor growth. The positive limiting ratios $Y/K$ and $X/Y$
in equations~\eqref{eq:rewrite-output-capital-limit} and
\eqref{eq:rewrite-ai-output-limit} imply a common limiting growth rate
of $2.1\%$ for the three normalized quantities, or $3.1\%$ for output
per person. At year 500, $Y/(AL)$ still grows at $2.2\%$: the path is
close to, but has not reached, its analytical limit.

I next examine wages and prices in
Figure~\ref{fig:rewrite-rsi-half-prices}. Panels A and B show real-wage
growth $g_w$ and the net interest rate $r$, in percent per year;
panel C shows the level of $p_X$, in final-good units on a logarithmic
scale. Unlike the quantity-growth panels, wage growth is not net of
$\gamma$. The upper and lower rows retain the initial and extended
time windows and the same four line styles.

Initially, wage growth rises in all four economies, but it is not ordered
monotonically by substitutability: the immediate increase is larger at
$\sigma=1$ than at $\sigma=1.50$. Equation~\eqref{eq:rewrite-wage}
explains why higher output per worker need not translate into equally
high wage growth: the labor share also changes. The interest rate starts
at $5.0\%$ in every case and then rises as output initially outpaces capital,
raising $Y/K$ in equation~\eqref{eq:rewrite-capital-return}.
This higher return also raises consumption growth through
equation~\eqref{eq:rewrite-euler}. Meanwhile, $p_X$ falls continuously
as AI efficiency improves. The pricing condition
\eqref{eq:rewrite-eq-monopoly} links the price to the compute cost per
service, $1/B$, and the markup. At unit elasticity the markup is constant,
so the price falls in inverse proportion to $B$; its decline over the
first 27 months is $39.6\%$, an outcome rather than a calibration target.

In the lower row, the three labor-bottleneck economies return toward wage
growth of $1.0\%$ and an interest rate of $5.0\%$, as stated in
equations~\eqref{eq:rewrite-complements-terminal}--\eqref{eq:rewrite-substitutes-labor-terminal}.
At $\sigma=1.50$, both rates rise above their long-run values before
declining. Equation~\eqref{eq:rewrite-substitutes-wage-growth} gives
limiting wage growth of $2.4\%$, below output-per-person growth of
$3.1\%$, while the limiting interest rate is
$\mathcal R(\overline B)=7.1\%$. At year 500, the corresponding wage
growth and interest rate are still $2.5\%$ and $7.2\%$.
The price approaches a positive level in every case, rather than falling
forever at a constant proportional rate. In the AI-dominated limit,
$s_X\to1$ and $e_X\to\alpha$, so the pricing condition gives
$p_X\to1/[(1-\alpha)\overline B]\simeq0.00110$.

Finally, I use Figure~\ref{fig:rewrite-rsi-half-distribution} to show
labor income $wL/Y$, developer net profit $\Pi/Y$, inference expenditure
$U/Y$, and research expenditure $M/Y$ in panels A--D. All values are
percentages of output. Here each time window occupies two rows: the
upper pair shows the initial transition and the lower pair shows years
10--500. Line styles again identify the four elasticities. The omitted
gross capital income share remains $\alpha=33.0\%$, as follows from
equation~\eqref{eq:rewrite-capital-return}.

In the upper panels, research spending jumps while labor income and
inference expenditure remain continuous at activation. Developer net
profit therefore falls by the new research expenditure, since
$\Pi=p_XX-U-M$ in equation~\eqref{eq:rewrite-eq-profit}. The largest
reallocation occurs at $\sigma=1.50$: $M/Y$ starts at $3.1\%$ and
$\Pi/Y$ falls from $3.3\%$ to $0.2\%$. Net profit remains positive
in this exercise. As the initial research share declines and AI sales
expand, profit recovers. Labor's share changes more gradually: over the
first decade it rises slightly under complementarity, stays at
$60.3\%$ at unit elasticity, and falls in the two substitution cases.
The direction follows from equations~\eqref{eq:rewrite-sx} and
\eqref{eq:rewrite-wage} as AI services expand relative to effective labor.

Over the longer horizon, only the $\sigma=1.50$ path loses its labor
bottleneck. Labor's share reaches half its initial value after about
47 years and is $0.2\%$ at year 500; the other cases retain positive
shares. Remark~\ref{rem:rewrite-ai-revenue-share} links this decline to
the output--wage growth gap in the preceding figures and implies that
AI revenue approaches $1-\alpha=67.0\%$ of output. That revenue is not
all profit. Combining the monopoly condition with
equation~\eqref{eq:rewrite-ai-output-limit} gives
$U/Y\to(1-\alpha)^2=44.9\%$; as the remaining scope for efficiency
improvement vanishes, $M/Y\to0$ and net profit approaches
$\alpha(1-\alpha)=22.1\%$. The persistent growth of AI services in
Figure~\ref{fig:rewrite-rsi-half-accumulation} is therefore ultimately
supported by expanding inference compute, not by a permanently high
research share.
