\subsection{A lower research-expenditure calibration}
\label{subsec:rewrite-rsi-research-share}

I now use research expenditure rather than the decline in the AI-service
price to guide the choice of $\chi$. The lower reference is the United
States in 2023: \citet{korinekmckelvey2026} estimate training compute
expenditure of \$18.46 billion, equivalent to $0.0664\%$ of nominal
GDP.\footnote{Training compute is from Table 3 of
\citet{korinekmckelvey2026}. Nominal GDP of \$27,811.517 billion is the
2023 value of the BEA series \texttt{GDPA}, available through
\href{https://fred.stlouisfed.org/data/GDPA}{FRED}, accessed September 14, 2026.}
This is the lowest of the 2023--2025 estimates considered, not a lower
confidence bound or an estimate of current research spending. The compute
estimate uses rental-equivalent costs and assigns half of AI compute
spending to training and research. It proxies $M$ but does not measure
autonomous RSI directly.

This exercise compares $\sigma=0.90,1,1.10,1.50$, starting each economy
on its fixed-efficiency pre-RSI BGP from
Section~\ref{subsec:rewrite-rsi-activation}. AI is already used in
production, $B_0=0.01\overline B$, and initial capital satisfies
$K_0/Y_0=3.30$. RSI becomes available unexpectedly at date zero, without
changing the initial stocks, production quantities or prices. Consumption
and research adjust endogenously. The initial capital stocks differ across
elasticities to preserve the common capital--output ratio, not to fit
post-activation outcomes. All parameters except $\chi$ remain unchanged
relative to the preceding exercises;
Table~\ref{tab:rewrite-research-share-parameters} lists them.

At $\sigma=1$, I compare annual expenditure with annual output:
\begin{equation}
 \frac{\int_0^1 M_t\,\dd t}{\int_0^1 Y_t\,\dd t}
 \simeq \frac{18.46}{27{,}811.517}=0.00066375.
 \label{eq:rewrite-research-share-target}
\end{equation}
Among the previously explored candidates on the increasing low-$\chi$
branch, $\chi=1.4378$ gives the closest annual ratio, $0.06374\%$.
It falls short of the empirical proxy by $0.26$ basis points, or about
$4.0\%$ of the target. I retain this approximate match rather than refine
$\chi$ to fit an uncertain proxy more closely. This choice does not relax
the equilibrium checks in Appendix~\ref{app:rewrite-algorithm}.
I then hold $\chi=1.4378$ fixed across the other three elasticities.
Their research expenditure and price declines are equilibrium outcomes,
not separately fitted targets.

The initial growth effects are modest across all four elasticities.
Output per person begins growing at instantaneous annual rates of
$1.08\%$, $1.23\%$, $1.26\%$, and $1.45\%$, compared with $1\%$ before
activation. At $\sigma=1.50$, consumption rises by $0.84\%$, research
initially absorbs $0.57\%$ of output, and developer net profit is positive
at $2.73\%$ of output. Gross investment remains $19.57\%$ of output:
physical capital grows by $0.93\%$ annually at activation, although it
falls relative to effective labor, which grows by $1.30\%$.
This adjustment requires neither initial developer losses nor capital
liquidation.

The first-year research shares are $0.00687\%$, $0.06374\%$, $0.14175\%$,
and $0.56313\%$ of output. Only the second value is calibrated: stronger
substitution changes the endogenous incentive to invest in research.
The untargeted price declines over 27 months are approximately $2.4\%$,
$9.0\%$, $10.9\%$, and $14.2\%$. Research expenditure relative to output
falls between the first and second years in all four cases, so matching
its level at unit elasticity does not reproduce its recent empirical growth.

Figures~\ref{fig:rewrite-research-share-accumulation}--\ref{fig:rewrite-research-share-distribution}
retain the same panels and time windows. The subsequent paths separate:
the three labor-supported economies return to output-per-person and
real-wage growth of $1\%$ and an interest rate of $5\%$. At $\sigma=1.50$,
growth accelerates and labor's income share tends to zero. Its decline
reaches half its initial share after approximately 221 model years,
compared with 47 in the half-price-decline exercise. The limiting
output-per-person growth, wage growth and interest rate remain $3.13\%$,
$2.42\%$, and $7.13\%$: changing $\chi$ changes the transition, not these
long-run outcomes.

The plotted horizon is not the terminal boundary. At year 500 in the
$\sigma=1.50$ case, output-per-person growth is $4.27\%$, wage growth is
$3.19\%$ and the interest rate is $8.20\%$, still above their limits;
labor's income share is $2.64\%$. The numerical solution is extended far
beyond the displayed window to verify convergence. At unit elasticity,
$B/\overline B$ is only $0.46$ at year 500, while labor's income share
remains $60.3\%$. A slow approach to the efficiency bound need not imply
a large remaining deviation of growth from its limiting rate.

\paragraph{Interpretation and limitations.}
These are quantitative scenarios informed by data, not a joint empirical
fit of the current AI economy. At unit elasticity, AI-industry revenue
is $(1-\alpha)\omega_X=6.7\%$ of output, independently of $K_0$, $B_0$
and $\chi$. That share is not fitted to the size estimates discussed by
\citet{korinekmckelvey2026}. Matching historical research expenditure also
does not reproduce its recent growth. The proxy measures
US training compute, not worldwide autonomous RSI. Nor does the exercise
match expenditure and price changes simultaneously.

The adjustment mechanism also limits the interpretation of timing.
RSI appears unexpectedly, compute use can expand without installation
delays, capital is reversible, and owners can finance developer losses
against future profit. The large initial reallocations in
Section~\ref{subsec:rewrite-rsi-activation}, including negative gross
investment, expose the importance of these assumptions. Lowering $\chi$
moderates the response but does not introduce the capacity costs or
complementary investments discussed by \citet{erdiletal2025gate} and
\citet{brynjolfssonrocksyverson2021}. Permanent monopoly and the absence
of human research further restrict the interpretation of prices and
developer profit.

Finally, $\sigma$ and $\overline B$ describe technological scenarios,
not estimated probabilities: the upper bound deliberately places
$\sigma=1.50$ in the AI-dominated regime. Transition dates are therefore
conditional model outcomes, not forecasts. The income shares describe
payments to labor, capital and the AI developer. Without heterogeneous
asset ownership or the creation of new human tasks, they do not establish
how gains are distributed across households or how political power changes.
