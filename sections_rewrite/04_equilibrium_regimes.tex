\section{Equilibrium existence with a finite AI-efficiency frontier}
\label{sec:rewrite-regimes}

I now give sufficient conditions for an infinite-horizon equilibrium in each
substitution regime. Throughout this section, $0<B_0<\overline B<\infty$,
$\chi>0$, $n+\gamma>0$, $\rho>n$, $0<\eta<\alpha<1$, and $2\eta\leq1$;
the remaining parameters satisfy Section~\ref{sec:rewrite-model}. These
results do not concern the uncapped economy. They are local in initial
stocks, not in time: each constructs a trajectory defined for every
$t\geq0$, including both transversality conditions and agent optimality.
The initial stocks need not lie on a balanced-growth path.

A finite AI-efficiency frontier does not necessarily make long-run growth of
output per person equal to $\gamma$. It bounds AI efficiency, but not the
compute used to supply effective AI production services. If AI and labor are substitutes, capital
and compute can sustain growth faster than effective labor even when
AI efficiency converges to a finite limit.

For any positive variable $V$, write $g_V=\dot V/V$. Define the
output--capital ratio required for growth at the effective-labor rate as
\begin{equation}
 \mathcal R=\frac{\rho+\gamma+\delta}{\alpha}.
 \label{eq:rewrite-required-output-capital}
\end{equation}
Indeed, if $K$ and $C$ grow at $n+\gamma$, the Euler equation requires
$r=\rho+\gamma$. For $\sigma\ne1$, define
\begin{align}
 z_A(\overline B;\sigma)
 &=\left[(1-\alpha)^2\overline B\,
 \omega_X^{\sigma/(\sigma-1)}\right]^{(1-\alpha)/\alpha},
 \label{eq:rewrite-ai-terminal-ratio}\\
 \overline B_c(\sigma)
 &=\frac{\mathcal R^{\alpha/(1-\alpha)}}
 {(1-\alpha)^2\omega_X^{\sigma/(\sigma-1)}}.
 \label{eq:rewrite-critical-frontier}
\end{align}
The first expression is the limiting $Y/K$ implied by the static equations
when $s_X\to1$ and $B\to\overline B$; the second solves
$z_A(\overline B_c;\sigma)=\mathcal R$. Appendix~\ref{app:rewrite-finite-existence}
derives these expressions and proves the following propositions. I use
$\overline B_c(\sigma)$ as an economic regime threshold only when $\sigma>1$.
For $\sigma<1$, it appears only as a sufficient condition for the particular
local finite-frontier construction below.

\subsection{Complementarity: \texorpdfstring{\(\sigma<1\)}{sigma < 1}}
\label{subsec:rewrite-complements}

\begin{proposition}[Equilibrium trajectories with complementary inputs]
\label{prop:rewrite-capped-complements-existence}
Maintain the common conditions of this section. If $0<\sigma<1$ and
$\overline B>\overline B_c(\sigma)$, there exist constants
$\overline k,\overline d>0$ and a neighborhood $\mathcal U_L$ of
$(\overline k,\overline d,0)$ such that every positive initial configuration
satisfying
\begin{equation}
 \left(\frac{K_0}{A_0N_0},\; (\overline B-B_0)A_0N_0,\;
 \frac{1}{A_0N_0}\right)\in\mathcal U_L
 \label{eq:rewrite-labor-local-initial}
\end{equation}
admits an interior infinite-horizon equilibrium. The initial values $C_0$
and $q_0$ are uniquely selected among nearby trajectories converging to this
terminal regime. Along the equilibrium,
\begin{equation}
 B\to\overline B,\qquad
 g_Y-n\to\gamma,\qquad g_w\to\gamma,\qquad
 r\to\rho+\gamma,\qquad
 \frac{wL}{Y}\to(1-\alpha)(1-\overline s_X)>0,
 \label{eq:rewrite-complements-terminal}
\end{equation}
where $\overline s_X\in(0,1)$ is the limiting CES share.
\end{proposition}

\Needspace{3\baselineskip}
With complementarity, both effective labor and effective AI production services remain relevant
in this terminal regime. Effective AI production services expand through compute even as
$g_B\to0$, and output per person eventually grows at $\gamma$. The frontier
condition ensures that AI efficiency is high enough to support this regime.
If it fails, the proposition does not establish nonexistence of equilibrium;
it only excludes the positive-ratio terminal construction used in its proof.

\subsection{Unit elasticity: \texorpdfstring{\(\sigma=1\)}{sigma = 1}}
\label{subsec:rewrite-unit}

At unit elasticity I use the continuous CES limit
$Z=(AL)^{\omega_L}X^{\omega_X}$, so $s_X=\omega_X$. Define
$\beta=(1-\alpha)\omega_X$. The monopoly condition gives $U=\beta^2Y$.

\begin{proposition}[Equilibrium trajectories at unit elasticity]
\label{prop:rewrite-capped-unit-existence}
Maintain the common conditions of this section and let $\sigma=1$.
For every finite $\overline B>0$, there is an interior infinite-horizon
equilibrium for initial configurations satisfying
\eqref{eq:rewrite-labor-local-initial} in a sufficiently small neighborhood,
with
\begin{equation}
 \overline k=
 \left[(\beta^2\overline B)^\beta
 \mathcal R^{\beta-1}\right]^{1/(1-\alpha-\beta)}.
 \label{eq:rewrite-unit-terminal-capital}
\end{equation}
The positive constant $\overline d$ is constructed in the proof. The nearby
convergent choice of $C_0,q_0$ is unique, and
\begin{equation}
 B\to\overline B,\qquad
 g_Y-n\to\gamma,\qquad g_w\to\gamma,\qquad
 r\to\rho+\gamma,\qquad
 \frac{wL}{Y}=(1-\alpha)\omega_L>0.
 \label{eq:rewrite-unit-terminal}
\end{equation}
\end{proposition}

The denominator in \eqref{eq:rewrite-unit-terminal-capital} is positive
because $1-\alpha-\beta=(1-\alpha)\omega_L>0$. No additional lower or
upper bound on the finite frontier is needed for this local construction.
The result describes transitions toward balanced growth, not an assumption
that the economy starts on a balanced-growth path.

\subsection{Gross substitution: \texorpdfstring{\(\sigma>1\)}{sigma > 1}}
\label{subsec:rewrite-substitutes}

\begin{proposition}[Equilibrium trajectories with substitute inputs]
\label{prop:rewrite-capped-substitutes-existence}
Maintain the common conditions of this section and let $\sigma>1$.

If $\overline B<\overline B_c(\sigma)$, initial configurations satisfying
\eqref{eq:rewrite-labor-local-initial} in a sufficiently small neighborhood
admit an interior infinite-horizon equilibrium with the limits in
\eqref{eq:rewrite-complements-terminal}, for a limiting share
$\overline s_X\in(0,1)$.

If $\overline B>\overline B_c(\sigma)$, let
$\varphi=(\sigma-1)/\sigma$ and define
\begin{equation}
 \overline r=\alpha z_A(\overline B;\sigma)-\delta,
 \qquad \overline g=n+\overline r-\rho>n+\gamma.
 \label{eq:rewrite-substitutes-terminal-growth}
\end{equation}
There exist a constant $\overline d>0$ and a neighborhood $\mathcal U_A$ of
$(0,\overline d,0)$ such that every positive initial configuration satisfying
\begin{equation}
 \left[\left(\frac{A_0N_0}{K_0}\right)^\varphi,\;
 (\overline B-B_0)K_0,\; \frac{1}{A_0N_0}\right]\in\mathcal U_A
 \label{eq:rewrite-ai-local-initial}
\end{equation}
admits an interior infinite-horizon equilibrium with
\begin{align}
 B&\to\overline B,& g_Y-n&\to\overline r-\rho>\gamma,&
 \frac{wL}{Y}&\to0,
 \label{eq:rewrite-substitutes-ai-limits}\\
 g_w&\to\gamma+
 \frac{\overline r-\rho-\gamma}{\sigma}>\gamma.
 \label{eq:rewrite-substitutes-wage-growth}
\end{align}
In both cases the nearby convergent choice of $C_0,q_0$ is unique.
The equality $\overline B=\overline B_c(\sigma)$ is not covered.
\end{proposition}

Above the threshold, a fixed AI-efficiency level allows output, capital, and
inference compute to grow faster than effective labor. Production becomes
asymptotically linear in capital, as in an $AK$ model. Growth is finite for
every fixed frontier, but need not equal $\gamma$. Real wages rise even as
labor's income share vanishes. Below the threshold, labor retains a positive
income share and long-run growth per person equals $\gamma$.

\begin{proposition}[The output--wage growth wedge]
\label{prop:rewrite-output-wage-wedge}
Along an AI-dominated equilibrium from
Proposition~\ref{prop:rewrite-capped-substitutes-existence}, output per person
grows asymptotically faster than the real wage:
\begin{equation}
 \lim_{t\to\infty}\bigl[(g_Y-n)-g_w\bigr]
 =\left(1-\frac{1}{\sigma}\right)
  (\overline r-\rho-\gamma)>0.
 \label{eq:rewrite-output-wage-wedge}
\end{equation}
Because $L=N$, the same wedge is the asymptotic rate at which labor's income
share falls:
\begin{equation}
 \lim_{t\to\infty}\frac{\mathrm d}{\mathrm dt}
 \log\left(\frac{wL}{Y}\right)
 =-\left(1-\frac{1}{\sigma}\right)
  (\overline r-\rho-\gamma)<0.
 \label{eq:rewrite-labor-share-decline-rate}
\end{equation}
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Subtract the wage-growth limit in
\eqref{eq:rewrite-substitutes-wage-growth} from the per-person output-growth
limit in \eqref{eq:rewrite-substitutes-ai-limits}. Moreover,
$\mathrm d\log(wL/Y)/\mathrm dt=g_w+n-g_Y$ because $L=N$.
\end{proof}

The result is not that workers must become poorer. Their real wage grows,
but total output per person grows faster. Labor therefore receives a
vanishing share of an expanding economy. The wedge is zero in the
positive-labor-share regimes, where both growth rates converge to $\gamma$.
Thus $\sigma>1$ alone is insufficient: the frontier must also place the
economy in the AI-dominated regime.

\subsection{Deriving long-run growth and the effect of the frontier}
\label{subsec:rewrite-growth-frontier}

The constructed trajectories have convergent growth rates and a positive
limiting consumption--output ratio. Write $\overline r=\lim_{t\to\infty}r_t$.
Consumption and output therefore have the same limiting growth rate.
The household Euler equation then gives
\begin{equation}
 \lim_{t\to\infty}\frac{\mathrm d\log(Y_t/N_t)}{\mathrm dt}
 =\lim_{t\to\infty}(g_C-n)=\overline r-\rho.
 \label{eq:rewrite-long-run-growth-return}
\end{equation}
In the positive labor-share regimes, $Y/(AN)$ converges to a positive
constant and its growth rate converges to zero. Consequently,
\begin{equation}
 g_Y\to n+\gamma,\qquad g_Y-n\to\gamma,
 \qquad \overline r=\rho+\gamma.
 \label{eq:rewrite-labor-growth-derivation}
\end{equation}
These are limits of the constructed transitions, not restrictions imposed
on their growth rates at every date.

In the AI-dominated regime, $s_X\to1$ and $e_X\to\alpha$. The CES function
and the monopoly condition imply
\begin{equation}
 \frac ZX\to\omega_X^{\sigma/(\sigma-1)},\qquad
 \frac UY\to(1-\alpha)^2,\qquad
 \frac XY\to\overline B(1-\alpha)^2.
 \label{eq:rewrite-ai-growth-static-limits}
\end{equation}
Production gives the identity $Y/K=(Z/Y)^{(1-\alpha)/\alpha}$.
Substitution of these limits therefore yields
\begin{equation}
 \frac YK\to z_A(\overline B;\sigma),\qquad
 \overline r=\alpha z_A(\overline B;\sigma)-\delta,\qquad
 \lim(g_Y-n)=\alpha z_A(\overline B;\sigma)-\delta-\rho.
 \label{eq:rewrite-ai-growth-derivation}
\end{equation}
The last expression exceeds $\gamma$ exactly when
$\overline B>\overline B_c(\sigma)$. A finite AI-efficiency level can thus
support sustained growth above $\gamma$: inference compute expands with
output, and effective labor ceases to constrain production asymptotically.

\Needspace{4\baselineskip}
Holding $\sigma>1$ and all other parameters fixed, the constructed families
give the following comparative statics across finite frontiers:
\begin{equation}
 \overline r(\overline B;\sigma)=
 \begin{cases}
  \rho+\gamma,&0<\overline B<\overline B_c(\sigma),\\[2pt]
  \alpha z_A(\overline B;\sigma)-\delta,
  &\overline B>\overline B_c(\sigma).
 \end{cases}
 \label{eq:rewrite-interest-frontier}
\end{equation}
Below the threshold, a larger frontier does not change the limiting
interest or growth rate. Above it,
\begin{equation}
 \frac{\partial\overline r}{\partial\overline B}
 =\frac{1-\alpha}{\overline B}z_A(\overline B;\sigma)>0,
 \qquad
 \lim_{\overline B\to\infty}\overline r(\overline B;\sigma)=\infty.
 \label{eq:rewrite-interest-frontier-derivative}
\end{equation}
The limiting growth rate per person has the same derivative because it
equals $\overline r-\rho$. The two interest-rate formulas approach
$\rho+\gamma$ at the threshold, although the existence proof does not cover
equality. For $\sigma=1$, and for the constructed complementary-input
family, $\overline r=\rho+\gamma$ instead remains independent of the frontier.

\subsection{Scope of the existence results}
\label{subsec:rewrite-regime-synthesis}

Figure~\ref{fig:rewrite-frontier-regimes} summarizes the central comparison
for $\sigma>1$. Here the finite frontier is an analytical device that bounds
recursive AI improvement. A frontier below $\overline B_c(\sigma)$ supports the
labor-bottleneck equilibrium: output per person and the real wage grow at
$\gamma$, and labor retains a positive income share. Above the threshold,
capital and effective AI production services grow faster than effective labor.
Output per person then grows faster than $\gamma$ and labor's income share
converges to zero. The figure omits $\sigma<1$ because I do not interpret
$\overline B_c(\sigma)$ as a regime boundary under complementarity.

Table~\ref{tab:rewrite-finite-existence} collects the sufficient frontier
conditions. Every row also requires the common parameter restrictions and
the corresponding initial-stock neighborhood. These are not necessary
conditions for all possible equilibria, nor a claim of global uniqueness.

The proof constructs convergent trajectories from nearby predetermined
stocks and then verifies both agents' optimality and both transversality
conditions. Research remains positive at every finite date. The constants
and neighborhoods depend on the parameters; the result does not establish
equilibrium from arbitrary $K_0,B_0,A_0,N_0$, at the threshold, or after
taking $\overline B\to\infty$. Those are separate questions.

The analytical limits do not require simulations. They do not, however,
determine transition duration or the paths of wages, interest rates, and
income shares. Claims about a prolonged moderate-growth phase require
additional analysis of equilibrium transitions.

\subsection{The uncapped economy and the role of the frontier}
\label{subsec:rewrite-uncapped}

The comparative static in
\eqref{eq:rewrite-interest-frontier-derivative} does not solve the economy
with an infinite AI-efficiency frontier. It first fixes a finite
$\overline B$, constructs an infinite-horizon equilibrium, takes that
economy's long-run limit, and only then compares the resulting limits as
$\overline B$ increases. Setting $\overline B=\infty$ at the outset changes
the state equation by setting $\psi(B)=1$ and removes the bound on the
developer's state. In general,
\begin{equation}
 \lim_{\overline B\to\infty}\lim_{t\to\infty}r_t(\overline B)
 \quad\text{need not equal}\quad
 \lim_{t\to\infty}r_t(\infty),
 \label{eq:rewrite-noncommuting-limits}
\end{equation}
and the expression on the right may not exist. The initial-stock
neighborhood and the equilibrium choices of $C_0$ and $q_0$ also depend on
the frontier. Existence for every fixed finite frontier therefore does not
imply existence in the uncapped economy.

This distinction is especially important when $\sigma>1$. At high AI
efficiency, effective AI production services can replace labor. Holding
current $K,A,$ and $L$ fixed, optimized operating profit then grows
asymptotically in proportion to $KB^{(1-\alpha)/\alpha}$. Research raises
$B$, and a higher $B$ both raises operating profit and makes subsequent
research compute more productive. The capped equilibria reveal the strength
of this feedback: once the economy is in the AI-dominated regime, the
limiting interest and output-growth rates increase with $\overline B$ and
become unbounded as $\overline B\to\infty$. This divergence is evidence that
the uncapped problem may behave qualitatively differently. It is not, by
itself, proof of a finite-time singularity or of equilibrium nonexistence.
No analogous divergence appears in the finite-frontier equilibria constructed
for $\sigma\leq1$, where labor remains a long-run bottleneck. This does not
establish existence without a frontier in those cases either.

The developer's optimization problem identifies a more immediate possible
failure. Proposition~\ref{prop:rewrite-research-scale} in
Appendix~\ref{app:rewrite-research-scale} shows that, in the uncapped economy,
the maintained restriction $\eta<\alpha$ makes the benefit generated by a
large research expenditure grow at most sublinearly in that expenditure on
a fixed finite horizon, while its cost grows linearly. It therefore rules out
an arbitrarily large research burst as the developer's optimal response in
that partial-equilibrium problem. If instead $\sigma>1$ and $\eta>\alpha$,
the developer can make its finite-horizon payoff arbitrarily large by
increasing an early research burst. No finite research plan maximizes its
objective. At $\eta=\alpha$, the outcome depends on the coefficients of the
leading benefit and cost terms. These results explain why $\eta<\alpha$ is a
substantive restriction, but they still do not establish an uncapped
infinite-horizon general equilibrium.

The failure when the developer's value is unbounded is economic rather than
numerical. Research compute $M$ is a use of the final good, not merely a
financial position. The household may finance a negative distribution from
the developer, but finance cannot create the real resources used in research.
Equation~\eqref{eq:rewrite-eq-kdot} requires research to compete with
consumption, inference compute, and physical investment. If every finite
research plan can be improved by choosing a larger research burst, no
allocation with finite resources can satisfy the developer's optimality
condition and the aggregate resource constraint at the same time. Such an
allocation is not an equilibrium under
Definition~\ref{def:rewrite-equilibrium}.

A different failure can occur even when the developer's value is bounded on
every finite horizon. Recursive improvement may cause $B$, output, capital,
or research expenditure to become unbounded at a finite date. I use
\emph{finite-time singularity} for this event, rather than for merely rapid
growth. A path that ends at such a date is not an infinite-horizon equilibrium
as defined in Section~\ref{sec:rewrite-model}: it does not provide finite,
feasible allocations for every $t\geq0$, and its infinite-horizon optimality
and transversality conditions are not defined. The analysis in this paper
does not prove that every uncapped trajectory with $\sigma>1$ reaches such a
singularity. It also does not rule out an uncapped equilibrium that remains
finite at every finite date. Explosive-growth mechanisms in
\citet{nordhaus2021} and \citet{davidsonetal2026} motivate this question but
do not replace the missing equilibrium argument for the present model.

I therefore use a finite frontier as an analytical continuation device, not
as an estimate of a permanent technological ceiling. For each finite
$\overline B$, the term $1-B/\overline B$ keeps AI efficiency in a bounded
state space and makes it possible to establish and compute an equilibrium.
I then raise the frontier and examine how the equilibrium regime, growth,
interest rates, and income shares change. This strategy separates results
that hold for every fixed finite frontier from claims about an uncapped
economy. If the capped equilibria fail to converge, or their limiting growth
rates diverge, the correct conclusion is that this continuation does not
produce an uncapped equilibrium---not that a finite-time singularity has
already been proved.
