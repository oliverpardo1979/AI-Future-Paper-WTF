\subsection{Lower research productivity}
\label{subsec:rewrite-rsi-research-share}

I now set $\chi=1.5$, one fifth of its value in
Section~\ref{subsec:rewrite-rsi-half-decline}. All other parameters and
initial stocks remain unchanged, including the elasticity-specific
pre-RSI capital stocks in Table~\ref{tab:rewrite-rsi-parameters}.
Consumption and research are solved again for each economy; the new
trajectories are not time-rescaled versions of the preceding exercise.

I begin with Figure~\ref{fig:rewrite-rsi-research-accumulation}. Panels A,
B, and C again report growth of $Y/(AL)$, $X/(AL)$, and $K/(AL)$,
in percent per year. The upper row shows years $-2$ to 10 and the lower
row years 10--500. Green dashed, black solid, orange dotted, and blue
dash-dotted lines retain their meanings: $\sigma=0.90,1,1.10,1.50$.
Black dotted horizontal lines show the unchanged $\sigma=1.50$ limits.
Vertical scales differ from the higher-productivity figures, so the
size of each response must be read from the axes.

In the upper row, initial output growth is moderate and closer across
the four economies. Growth of $Y/(AL)$ ranges from $0.1\%$ at
$\sigma=0.90$ to $0.5\%$ at $\sigma=1.50$, equivalent to
output-per-person growth of $1.1\%$--$1.5\%$.
AI services still expand faster than output, but their initial normalized
growth rate at $\sigma=1.50$ is $11.6\%$, rather than $80.8\%$.
Lower research productivity slows efficiency improvements through
equation~\eqref{eq:rewrite-capability-law}; the developer also chooses
a different research path under equation~\eqref{eq:rewrite-research-foc}.
Consumption still rises at activation and research absorbs resources,
so $K/(AL)$ initially falls, but less than in the preceding exercise.
Capital subsequently accumulates faster than effective labor, allowing
normalized output growth to rise during the first decade even though
AI-service growth is already slowing.

In the lower row, the $\sigma=1.50$ path separates much later from the
labor-bottleneck cases. Output and capital growth rise for several
centuries, while AI-service growth first declines and then rises again
as capital and inference use expand. By year 500, $Y/(AL)$ grows at
$3.2\%$, still well above its $2.1\%$ analytical limit. It is then
growing faster than in the higher-productivity exercise because it is
at a different stage of adjustment, not because lower $\chi$ raises
long-run growth. Proposition~\ref{prop:rewrite-equilibrium-regimes}
still gives zero limiting normalized growth at $\sigma=0.90,1,1.10$
and positive growth at $\sigma=1.50$. The common efficiency bound and
production parameters, not the speed of research, determine those limits.

Figure~\ref{fig:rewrite-rsi-research-prices} shows the corresponding
real-wage growth, net interest rate, and AI-service price in panels A--C.
The first two are annual percentage rates; $p_X$ is a level in final-good
units on a logarithmic scale. The upper and lower rows use the same
initial and extended horizons, with the same scenario lines.

Initially, wage growth remains close across scenarios and interest rates
rise only gradually from $5.0\%$. At $\sigma=1.50$, the interest rate
reaches $5.5\%$ after ten years, compared with $7.8\%$ under higher
research productivity. The smaller increase in $Y/K$ explains the
smaller return response through equation~\eqref{eq:rewrite-capital-return}.
Price declines are also slower: at $\sigma=1$, $p_X$ falls by $9.4\%$
over 27 months, rather than $39.6\%$. With the same constant markup
at unit elasticity, this comparison directly reflects slower efficiency
improvement. These price changes are outcomes, not fitted moments.

In the lower row, wages and returns in the labor-bottleneck cases
gradually approach the $1.0\%$ and $5.0\%$ limits from Section~\ref{sec:rewrite-regimes}.
The AI-dominated case instead displays a delayed rise and subsequent
decline in both rates. At year 500, wage growth is $3.1\%$ and the
interest rate is $8.2\%$, still above the unchanged limits of $2.4\%$
and $7.1\%$. Output per person is then growing at $4.2\%$, faster
than wages. Equations~\eqref{eq:rewrite-substitutes-ai-limits} and
\eqref{eq:rewrite-substitutes-wage-growth} imply that this growth gap
persists in the limit, even after both rates fall further.
The AI-service price is already close to its positive limit at year 500,
but that proximity does not imply that capital accumulation, wages,
or the interest rate have completed their adjustment.

I complete the comparison with
Figure~\ref{fig:rewrite-rsi-research-distribution}. Panels A--D report
$wL/Y$, $\Pi/Y$, $U/Y$, and $M/Y$, all as percentages of output.
The upper two rows cover the initial transition and the lower two
cover years 10--500. The four line styles identify the same elasticities,
and the omitted gross capital share is again $33.0\%$.

In the upper panels, the initial research allocation and reduction in
net profit are smaller. At $\sigma=1.50$, research expenditure starts
at $0.6\%$ of output, leaving net profit at $2.7\%$, rather than
$0.2\%$ under higher research productivity. This is the same accounting
reallocation in equation~\eqref{eq:rewrite-eq-profit}, but on a smaller
scale. At unit elasticity, first-year research expenditure is $0.1\%$
of output, compared with $0.2\%$ in the preceding exercise; these
shares divide total research expenditure by total output over the first
year, rather than measuring $M_0/Y_0$.
Labor's share at $\sigma=1.50$ falls from $61.9\%$ to $60.1\%$
over ten years, compared with $53.0\%$ at year ten under higher
research productivity. Thus the first decade gives little indication
of how different the eventual income distribution will be.

The lower panels reveal that difference. Labor's share reaches half its
initial value after about 213 years, rather than 47, and is still
$2.3\%$ at year 500. Research's share initially declines, then rises
as the economy expands, and eventually falls as efficiency approaches
its bound. This nonmonotonic path reflects both the changing value of
efficiency improvements and the diminishing room for further gains in
equations~\eqref{eq:rewrite-research-foc} and
\eqref{eq:rewrite-capability-return}; lower $\chi$ does not merely
stretch the earlier path along the time axis.
The long-run accounting remains unchanged: in the AI-dominated case,
labor's share tends to zero, inference expenditure to $44.9\%$ of
output, net profit to $22.1\%$, and research expenditure relative to
output to zero. The other three economies retain a positive labor
share, as in Proposition~\ref{prop:rewrite-equilibrium-regimes}.
Lower research productivity therefore postpones the growth and
distributional transition without changing the eventual regime.
These are conditional model outcomes, not forecasts of takeoff dates.
