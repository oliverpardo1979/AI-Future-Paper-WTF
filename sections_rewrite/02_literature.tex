\section{Related literature}
\label{sec:rewrite-literature}

\paragraph{AI, growth, and automated research.}
The research technology builds on the endogenous- and semi-endogenous-growth
tradition, in which nonrival knowledge and resources devoted to ideas determine
long-run growth \citep{romer1990,aghionhowitt1992,jones1995}. Recent work asks
how the conclusion changes when AI can automate production, research, or both.
\citet{aghionjonesjones2019} show that automating idea production can accelerate
growth but that tasks that remain difficult to automate can become binding
bottlenecks. \citet{trammellkorinek2023} distinguish the automation of ordinary
production from the automation of R\&D, while \citet{jones2025rd} emphasizes the
share of research tasks that AI can perform, its productivity in those tasks,
and the remaining bottlenecks. \citet{mullainathanrambachan2025} argue that AI
may also reorganize the scientific process by bringing idea generation, theory
selection, and other research activities that currently occur off screen into
algorithmic workflows. The closest feedback architecture is
\citet{davidsonetal2026}: more capable AI makes subsequent AI research more
productive, while higher output expands the resources that can finance that
research. I place these technological and economic feedbacks
inside a decentralized Ramsey economy in which saving, inference compute,
research compute, prices, and payments are equilibrium outcomes. This differs
from quantitative scenario models such as \citet{erdiletal2025gate}, which
combine AI development, automation, investment, and growth but abstract from
the decentralized price system and market structure studied here.

\paragraph{Substitution, bottlenecks, and long-run growth.}
The production side connects this literature to the classic result that
sufficient substitution toward accumulable inputs can prevent their marginal
product from vanishing and produce an asymptotically linear technology
\citep{pitchford1960,jonesmanuelli1990,rebelo1991}.
\citet{duffypapageorgiou2000} emphasize that a high elasticity is not by itself
sufficient: the productivity of the accumulable input must also be high enough.
In the AI literature, \citet{nordhaus2021} identifies substitutability
between information and conventional inputs as central to an economic
singularity. \citet{jonestonetti2026} show instead how a small number of weak
links can delay growth acceleration even when automation advances, and
\citet{restrepo2025agi} distinguishes work that remains a bottleneck from work
that is no longer essential for growth. I obtain a related distinction without
assuming that all work can be performed by AI. Gross substitution between AI
services and labor is necessary but not sufficient for labor to cease being a
bottleneck: attainable AI efficiency must also be high enough. I derive the
threshold separating the labor-bottleneck and AI-dominated equilibria. The
capability threshold in \citet{gans2025aiknowledge} concerns a different
mechanism---whether AI directs research toward dense or exploratory regions of
the knowledge space---rather than whether effective AI services can replace
labor in final production.

\paragraph{Wages and the labor income share.}
The distributional results build on the task approach to automation.
\citet{acemoglurestrepo2019} distinguish the displacement effect of automating
existing tasks from the productivity and reinstatement effects that can raise
labor demand. In CES models, the unit-elasticity threshold determines whether
greater use of an input reduces the income share of the other input, a mechanism
used by \citet{karabarbounisneiman2014} to study the declining labor share.
Recent work shows that greater automation can therefore raise wages while
reducing labor's income share \citep{autorkausik2026,yango2026}.
\citet{agrawalgansgoldfarb2025} show how greater AI efficiency reallocates
knowledge creation toward the hardest questions and can eventually displace
routine knowledge workers. In the long run,
\citet{raymookherjee2022} show that capital accumulation can drive labor's share
to zero while real wages rise indefinitely, and \citet{restrepo2025agi} obtains
a vanishing labor share when compute can perform all bottleneck work. I do not
claim either coexistence result as new. I show instead when it arises in the
equilibrium with autonomous AI research: above the regime threshold, output per
person and real wages both grow faster, but output grows faster than wages. The
result yields an exact expression linking the output--wage growth wedge to the
rate at which labor's income share declines. The possible erosion of labor and
human-consumption tax bases motivates the public-finance analysis in
\citet{korineklockwood2026}. I do not study taxation, but the regime results
identify when that policy problem can arise.

\paragraph{Interest rates.}
AI-driven growth can also affect equilibrium returns. Faster expected
consumption growth raises long-term real rates through consumption smoothing in
\citet{chowhalperinmazlish2026}, and the AI scenarios in \citet{rachel2025}
and \citet{melinavilla2025} can raise the natural rate through different saving
and investment channels. The sign is not universal in richer models. I study a
narrower object: the net return on physical capital in a closed-economy Ramsey
equilibrium. The return remains tied to labor-augmenting technological progress
when labor is the bottleneck, but rises with attainable AI efficiency in the
AI-dominated regime. This is a regime-specific comparative static, not a
forecast of market interest rates.

\paragraph{Measurement of AI services.}
The distinction between raw compute and the effective services it produces is
informed by \citet{korinekmckelvey2026}, who separately measure physical
compute, inference, training and R\&D, algorithms, and model capital. I retain
separate compute flows for inference and research but summarize the technology
that augments both in one AI-efficiency stock. \citet{coylepoquiz2025} document
the broader difficulty of measuring AI investment, inputs, outputs, quality
change, and changes in production processes. These measurement problems caution
against interpreting the model's AI-efficiency stock as a directly observed
asset.

\paragraph{Market structure and economic power.}
The integrated developer is a tractable limiting case of the concentration
forces studied by \citet{korinekvipra2025} and the AI market-power questions
surveyed by \citet{gans2024}. \citet{atheyscottmorton2025} model AI as a priced
intermediate input and show how upstream market power affects adoption,
downstream prices, factor returns, and welfare. Evidence on model pricing,
entry, and differentiation in \citet{demireretal2025} also cautions against
treating current AI markets as a literal monopoly. I hold market structure fixed
to isolate the dynamic mechanism. This accounting distinguishes the AI
industry's gross revenue from monopoly profit: inference and research
expenditure are paid before the developer distributes the residual to its
owner.

Market structure can matter beyond prices and measured income.
\citet{brynjolfssonhitzig2025} argue that AI can make centralized coordination
more effective, increasing firm concentration and centralizing economic and,
potentially, political power. Those channels lie outside my model. They explain,
however, why labor's income share remains informative even when real wages rise:
it measures how much of aggregate income accrues to human labor rather than to
asset owners. It is not, by itself, a measure of political power.

\paragraph{Contribution.}
The individual ingredients---research automation, production bottlenecks,
declining labor shares, rising wages, and higher returns under faster
growth---are established in the literature. My contribution is their joint
equilibrium characterization in a model with recursive AI improvement and a
profit-maximizing AI developer. I derive an explicit condition separating the
labor-bottleneck and AI-dominated regimes, characterize the associated growth,
wage, interest-rate, and income-share limits, and establish sufficient
conditions for nearby infinite-horizon equilibrium trajectories rather than
only balanced-growth candidates. The existence results are local in initial
stocks and apply to each fixed finite AI-efficiency frontier; they do not claim
global existence from arbitrary initial conditions or establish an equilibrium
for an unbounded frontier.
