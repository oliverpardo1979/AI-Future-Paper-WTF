\section{Related literature}
\label{sec:rewrite-literature}

I build on the feedback mechanisms studied by \citet{davidsonetal2026}
and \citet{jonestonetti2026}, in which technological progress improves
research and higher output expands the resources available for further
innovation. Both papers endogenize technological progress, but their growth
applications hold investment and research allocation shares fixed.
\citet{erdiletal2025gate} instead optimize investment and compute
allocation through a social planner's problem, with adjustment costs and
an optional wedge for research externalities. I model a monopolistic
developer that chooses research spending to maximize discounted net profit,
alongside a household that chooses saving. The distinction is how private
research incentives and AI pricing jointly determine growth, wages, returns
to physical capital, and developer profit.

Private incentives to innovate have precedents in \citet{romer1990},
where monopoly rents finance nonrival knowledge, and in
\citet{acemoglurestrepo2018} and \citet{hemousolsen2022}, where expected
profits direct innovation. Acemoglu and Restrepo distinguish research that
automates existing tasks from research that creates new tasks for labor.
I instead study investment in AI efficiency through autonomous research,
without introducing new human tasks. More directly, \citet{liuwan2026} connect AI industry
structure to innovation in a model linking general-purpose AI to
specialized applications. I hold market structure fixed and study an
integrated developer whose AI services support both final-good production
and further AI research. This allows me to distinguish the remuneration of
physical capital from the developer's net profit and to examine how private
RSI investment affects both sources of income.

The distinction between labor-bottleneck and AI-dominated growth builds on
\citet{aghionjonesjones2019}, who show how tasks that remain difficult to
automate can constrain growth even as other tasks become increasingly
automated. In my model, research is already autonomous, but labor can still
constrain final-good production. I characterize when AI improvements raise
growth temporarily and when they support a permanent increase. With a finite
upper bound on AI efficiency, substitution between AI and labor is not
sufficient for the latter: attainable efficiency must exceed a threshold.
Capital accumulation can then sustain faster growth even as AI efficiency
approaches its upper bound.

\citet{casellimanning2019} establish conditions for technological progress
to raise real wages once capital adjusts, comparing steady states at a
fixed interest rate. Rising wages alongside a vanishing labor share also
arise in \citet{raymookherjee2022}, where capital accumulation can drive
automation without technical progress. I study wage growth with an
endogenous interest rate and private RSI investment. With a finite
AI-efficiency upper bound, real wages grow faster than labor-augmenting
technological progress in the AI-dominated regime, but output per worker
grows even faster. An exact expression links this wage-growth premium to
the rate at which labor's income share declines. I distinguish AI revenue
from net profit after inference and research costs, and show how the
transition can involve substantial research expenditure and temporary
reductions in developer net profit.
